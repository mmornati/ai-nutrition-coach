# Turn honest numbers into one calm next step

Every mainstream nutrition app now runs the same machine (search, barcode, AI photo or voice, a nutrient database, a target, and some form of score), and almost all of them sell the useful parts behind a $40–100 a year paywall. None of them is a good coach. They grade food (Noom colours, WW points, Yuka's 0–100, Lifesum ratings), grade the person (Lifesum's weekly Life Score), or hand back raw numbers with little explanation (Cronometer, MacroFactor). The evidence points to a different product: show real grams and nutrients, but turn them into **one autonomy-supportive suggestion at a time**, framed as something to add or try, judged against weekly ranges from WHO, EFSA and the French PNNS rather than daily pass/fail. Behaviour goals, self-monitoring and feedback are the techniques with the strongest trial support; moral food labels, scores of the user and calorie-first framing carry documented eating-disorder risk. Our app's privacy model, open data stack (CIQUAL, USDA, Open Food Facts) and free bring-your-own-key AI already beat competitors on price and trust, but today it has no grams, no nutrients and a coach that almost always says "add greens". The decided Phases 2–4 close most of that gap. This report adds about 30 concrete items to those phases, proposes Phase 5 (logging convenience) and Phase 6 (a parent-run, number-free family mode), and sets one hard rule: **calorie, gram and weight coaching stays strictly for the adult's own log and never touches a child**.

## Every tracker runs the same loop, and the paywall decides who gets it

The 2026 baseline is search, barcode scanning, AI photo recognition and AI voice or text logging, all returning an editable result. MacroFactor has the most complete toolkit: AI "Snap" and "Describe" modes, a barcode scanner that falls back to a nutrition-label scanner on a miss, recipe import from URL, copy-paste of foods, meals or whole days, quick-add, speech-to-text and metric units ([MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactor/); [MacroFactor May 2026](https://macrofactor.com/mm-may-2026/)). Its AI splits a meal into **editable ingredients with gram amounts**, which is now the standard pattern. Yazio describes its own photo feature as a "rough estimate" and moved it from free to Pro ([Yazio Help](https://help.yazio.com/hc/en-us/articles/39137901903889-What-is-the-AI-Calorie-Tracking-Feature-and-how-does-it-work)). Lifesum's "Track with AI" accepts photo, voice, text or scan and is Premium ([Lifesum Help](https://help.lifesum.com/en/article/what-is-the-difference-between-ai-tracking-and-the-traditional-tracking-5l8fj8/)). Cronometer's Photo Logging is a Gold feature that pulls values from curated NCCDB and Canadian data ([Cronometer](https://cronometer.com/blog/photo-logging/)).

The paywall line is where these apps differ most. MyFitnessPal is the outlier: since October 2022 even **barcode scanning is Premium**, a move that drew strong backlash ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/phones/myfitnesspal-barcode-scanning-not-free-premium-subscription); [Pocket-lint](https://www.pocket-lint.com/apps/news/162386-wow-myfitnesspal-put-its-popular-barcode-scanner-feature-behind-a-paywall)). Its Premium page lists Barcode & Meal Scan, Voice Log and custom macro goals as paid, at €49.99 a year in the EU ([MFP Premium](https://www.myfitnesspal.com/premium)) and $79.99 in the US, with a $99.99 Premium+ tier that adds meal planning ([MFP blog](https://blog.myfitnesspal.com/myfitnesspal-membership-pricing-tiers/)). MacroFactor has no free tier at all ($71.99 a year) but no ads and exportable data ([MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactor/)). Everyone else gates AI. For an app that lets users bring their own key, this is the clearest price differentiator available: **free barcode and free AI at the cost of the user's own API calls**.

Databases split into two families. Crowdsourced ones (MyFitnessPal, Lose It!, FatSecret, largely Yazio and Lifesum) are large and noisy; curated ones (Cronometer's lab-analysed NCCDB/USDA/CNF data, MacroFactor's 1.36 million "verified" entries) are smaller and more reliable. Peer-reviewed validation finds the noise sits in specific nutrients: a MyFitnessPal study against a Belgian reference database found energy agreement of r=0.96 after cleaning but only **ρ=0.53 for sodium and ρ=0.51 for cholesterol** ([PMC7641788](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7641788/)), and a 2019 UK study found Lose It! and FatSecret underestimated protein and sodium ([JMIR mHealth 2019](https://doaj.org/article/e71f33ad55574fca8999bf4152317fb5)). Salt advice built on crowdsourced data needs a provenance label.

Targets are mostly static formulas, with one exception worth copying. MacroFactor estimates energy expenditure from logged intake and weight trend, adjusts weekly, "holds" when logging pauses, and calls its adjustments **"adherence neutral"**: it does not scold a missed day ([MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/macrofactor/)). That calculation needs no server and fits a local-first PWA.

Coaching, where it exists, is mostly scoring. Lifesum rates each food per 100 kcal and turns 16 measures into a weekly Life Score from 0 to 150 ([Lifesum help](https://help.lifesum.com/en/article/life-score-android-afv0fw/)). Noom colours foods green, yellow or orange by calorie density and stresses that orange is not off-limits ([Noom help](https://www.noom.com/support/faqs/using-the-app/logging-and-tracking/food-and-water/2025/10/how-nooms-food-color-system-works/)). WW collapses calories, saturated fat, sugar and protein into Points, with 150+ ZeroPoint foods ([CNN](https://edition-prod-cf.sitemirror.cnn.com/2025/05/06/business/weight-watchers-bankruptcy); [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/weightwatchers-nears-bankruptcy-filing-per-report/)), and filed for Chapter 11 in May 2025 as GLP-1 drugs drew members away ([CTV News](https://www.ctvnews.ca/health/article/weightwatchers-files-for-bankruptcy/)). Yuka's 0–100 score is 60% Nutri-Score, 30% its own additive scale and 10% organic status ([Mama Knows Nutrition](https://mamaknowsnutrition.com/yuka-app-reviews)). Noom backs human coaches with the Welli chatbot ([Noom via FinTech Futures](https://www.fintechfutures.com/press-releases/noom-introduces-ai-enabled-products-to-enhance-on-demand-health-care-and-interactive-coaching)). Nobody in the set delivers a coach that reads the whole day and says, calmly and specifically, what to change next.

Open-source peers (OpenNutriTracker, Waistline, Energize) all pair Open Food Facts with a government database, store data locally, and offer **no AI photo logging** ([Waistline](https://github.com/davidhealey/waistline); [linuxphoneapps Energize](https://linuxphoneapps.org/apps/com.flasskamp.energize/); [brightcoding](https://www.blog.brightcoding.dev/2026/09/29/simonoppowaopennutritracker-open-source-calorie-tracker-with-barcode-scanning)). None is a PWA and none coaches.

## Photo AI undercounts by 250–345 kcal, so grams and grounding matter more than the model

The most important finding for our photo feature is that **all commercial photo apps undercount, and they undercount fat**. In a preliminary NIH test presented at Nutrition 2026 (not yet peer-reviewed), MyFitnessPal, Lose It!, Cal AI and Appediet were run on photos of 100+ meals weighed to 0.1 g in a metabolic kitchen. All four underestimated by **roughly 250 to 345 kcal per meal**, mostly from about 30 g of missed fat, and did worst on high-fat keto meals ([HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/nutrition/how-accurate-are-photo-based-calorie-apps-4-are-put-to-the-test)). Cal AI, which markets "about 80%" or "90%" accuracy, had the largest undercount ([eesel.ai](https://www.eesel.ai/en/blog/cal-ai-pricing)).

General vision models, which is what a BYOK user calls, do no better. A 2025 study found GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet had **about 36% mean absolute percentage error** on weight and energy, protein error of 61% or worse, and negative bias slopes, meaning they underestimate more as portions grow ([PMC12513282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513282/)). A ChatGPT-4 study saw large meals estimated at 530 g against 798 g actual ([PMC11858203](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11858203/)). Two levers fix most of this. First, context: telling the model about hidden oil, sauce or filling raised energy R² from **0.59 to 0.94** ([Cinar et al. 2026](https://avesis.uludag.edu.tr/yayin/4dd659a6-bb7a-4945-a926-b7f043a9a7fd/image-based-nutritional-assessment-evaluating-the-performance-of-chatgpt-4o-on-simple-and-complex-meals)). Second, grounding: a 2026 Purdue pipeline had the model list items, verified each one, asked "what did I miss?", then mapped each item to a USDA food code for the numbers, cutting mass error from 114 g to 74 g with open-weight models ([arXiv 2610.11144](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11144)). SnapCalorie (USDA) and Cal AI (a 1M-food database) also take final values from a database, not from the model ([SnapCalorie App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1574239307)).

The design consequence is direct. The model should return items, gram estimates with a low–high range, preparation method, likely hidden fats and at most one or two multiple-choice clarifying questions ("fried or grilled?", "any dressing?"); the app looks up nutrients from a curated table; model-generated numbers are a flagged fallback. Large plates deserve a gram slider. Depth sensing (SnapCalorie's LiDAR) is native-only and not available to a Safari PWA, so user confirmation of grams carries the weight. This also gives the coach its first "fewer calories" lever: **the hidden-fat question is itself coaching**, because cooking oil is where the calories hide.

The open data stack is strong and legally simple. USDA FoodData Central is CC0 public domain, with a small Foundation Foods file and 22,046 FNDDS household portion weights ([FDC API guide](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide); [FNDDS contents](https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/2021_2023_Contents_of_Datasets.pdf)). CIQUAL 2025 covers 3,484 French foods and 74 constituents ([ANSES](https://www.anses.fr/en/content/new-enhanced-and-more-representative-version-ciqual-table)), though the exact 2025 licence wording was not confirmed. Open Food Facts is ODbL with share-alike for derived databases ([OFF terms](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)), so fetching and caching per user, rather than shipping a derived dump, keeps obligations simple.

## Coaching patterns worth adopting, each tied to evidence

The strongest evidence comes from a meta-regression of 48 RCTs in adults with overweight: **goal setting of behaviour and self-monitoring predicted effects both short and long term**, and long term, an autonomy-supportive, motivational-interviewing style plus feedback on behaviour explained all the between-study variation ([Samdal et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5370453)). Our logging already covers self-monitoring. What we lack is the goal, the feedback and the style. A review of 40 diet apps found they average 8 behaviour-change techniques, mostly "provide instruction" and "prompt self-monitoring", not the effective ones ([PMC4080693](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4080693)). That is the gap a coach can fill.

| Pattern to adopt | Evidence | How it shows up in our app | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| User-chosen behaviour goals, not outcome goals | Behaviour goal setting predicts short- and long-term effect ([Samdal 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5370453)) | "Vegetables at lunch on 4 days this week", picked by the user from suggestions; no weight goal required | P3 |
| Feedback on behaviour, not on the person | Feedback is one of three techniques explaining long-term variation ([Samdal 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5370453)) | "Fibre was around 18 g on most days; beans at lunch would add about 5 g", never "you failed" | P3 |
| Autonomy-supportive, MI-style tone | Predicts maintenance ([Samdal 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5370453)); gain frames help only when autonomy is primed, and both frames can trigger reactance ([PMC8430703](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8430703)) | "One idea, if you like"; LLM coach asks "what would feel doable this week?" | P3 |
| "Add" before "avoid" (gain framing) | Small but consistent edge for prevention behaviours, r≈0.08 across 94 studies ([PMC4117640](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4117640)) | "Add a fistful of beans" before "less pasta" | P1 patch, P3 |
| Energy density as a neutral fewer-calories lever | Lowering energy density cut intake about twice as much as cutting portions; combining both worked best ([Science News on Rolls](https://www.sciencenews.org/blog/food-thought/fooling-satiety-meter-recipe)); long-term weight effect probably modest ([Red Pen Reviews](https://www.redpenreviews.org/?p=1652)) | kcal per 100 g shown as "lighter / medium / denser", never green-to-red; "swap half the rice for vegetables keeps the plate the same size" | P2, P3 |

The thread through all of this is that "eat fewer calories" coaching works best when it rarely mentions calories. Energy density, the plate split, hidden fats and sugary drinks are the four levers that reduce intake without a deficit counter, and each can be phrased as an addition or a swap. Implementation-intention prompts ("when I make lunch, I'll add beans") are a reasonable extension, but the notes found no diet-app trial of them, so treat that as an experiment rather than an evidence-backed feature.

## Patterns to avoid: grades, guilt and paywalled cancellations have a documented cost

The case against moralising food is stronger than the case for any score. People implicitly link calorie-dense foods with sin across healthy, anorexic and orthorexic groups ([PMC9234570](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9234570/)), and the Alliance for Eating Disorders advises against good/bad labels because they add shame ([Alliance](https://www.allianceforeatingdisorders.com/removing-food-morality/)). Proprietary composite scores attract regulators and industry: Italy's competition authority reportedly called Yuka's judgements absolute and simplistic until Yuka changed the app ([Il Sole 24 Ore](https://en.ilsole24ore.com/art/yuka-app-like-nutriscore-penalises-italias-food-industry-AIynzfzB)), and the UK Food & Drink Federation said ZOE's score contradicts government advice ([Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/new-app-from-zoe-labels-food-risk-in-seconds/)).

Calorie tracking itself carries risk for a minority. In a clinical sample of about 105 people with eating disorders, **73% of MyFitnessPal users felt the app contributed to their disorder** ([PubMed 28843591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28843591/)); among undergraduates, calorie tracking was associated with food anxiety and restrictive dieting ([Psychology in Action](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/2018-4-29-calorie-counting-and-fitness-tracking-technology-associations-with-eating-disorder-symptomology/)). Both studies are cross-sectional and cannot show causation; people with symptoms may simply track more. That uncertainty argues for safeguards, not for hiding numbers from everyone. The Phase 0 decision to show real numbers is sound for adults, provided the safeguards ship with the numbers in Phase 2 rather than later.

Front-of-pack labels do shift purchases somewhat across 134 studies ([QMUL](https://www.qmul.ac.uk/news/latest-news/2021/smd/new-study-supports-call-for-mandatory-front-of-pack-labelling-to-improve-diets.html)), so showing Nutri-Score is not useless. The line we draw is between describing a product with a public standard and judging the person who ate it.

## Children need a family mode with no numbers at all

The tension in the brief resolves cleanly once the evidence is laid out: **"eat fewer calories" coaching must never apply to children**. No expert body consulted recommends calorie counting, weighing food or intake tracking for children. The AAP's 2016 report identifies dieting and weight talk as predictors of both obesity and eating disorders, advises families to talk about healthy eating instead of weight, and notes that girls who dieted in 9th grade were about three times more likely to be overweight by 12th grade ([AJMC on AAP 2016](https://ajmc.com/view/preventing-obesity-and-eating-disorders-in-teenagers)). The AAP's 2023 obesity guideline puts treatment in clinician-led family programmes of at least 26 contact hours, not apps ([AAFP editorial](https://aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2023/1000/editorial-aap-guideline-childhood-obesity.html)). After Beat's campaign, Public Health England moved to keep under-18s out of its own weight-loss app ([Beat](https://beateatingdisorders.org.uk/news/beat-campaigners-celebrate-improvement-in-nhs-app)), and 98% of people affected by eating disorders said calories on menus would harm them ([RCPsych](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/members/devolved-nations/rcpsych-in-northern-ireland/northern-ireland-news/detail/2022/08/23/98-of-people-affected-by-eating-disorders-feel-calories-on-menus-will-negatively-impact-them-new-survey-finds)). The evidence here is precautionary: no trial shows calorie apps cause eating disorders in under-18s. But the consensus is unanimous and the downside is severe, so the rule should be absolute.

Kurbo is the warning in one product. WW launched it in 2019 for ages 8–17 with traffic-light foods, paid coaching and before/after photos of children ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/ww-launches-kurbo-by-ww-to-help-kids-and-teens-reach-a-healthier-weight-through-a-scientifically-proven-program-virtual-coaching-and-a-free-app-300900812.html)). A petition gathered more than 78,000 signatures ([change.org](https://www.change.org/wakeupweightwatchers)). In 2022 the FTC and DOJ imposed a **$1.5M penalty and ordered deletion of children's data and of algorithms built from it**, citing a bypassable age gate, an unverifiable "I'm a parent" path and indefinite retention ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/03/ftc-takes-action-against-company-formerly-known-weight-watchers-illegally-collecting-kids-sensitive)). The app left Google Play in August 2022 ([AppBrain](https://www.appbrain.com/app/kurbo-by-ww-weight-watchers/org.kurbo.app)).

What children's guidance does support is structure that parents control. Ellyn Satter's Division of Responsibility gives the parent "what, when and where" and the child "how much and whether" ([Satter DOR handout](https://ar.childrenshospital.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/Framingham%20-%20Ellyn%20Satter%20Division%20of%20Responsibility.pdf)). Family meals are protective: girls with five or more a week had lower odds of extreme weight-control behaviours five years later (OR 0.71) ([PubMed 18180407](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18180407)). Repeated, unpressured exposure is the best-supported technique for getting young children to eat vegetables, with intake rising with each exposure and plain vegetables working best ([PubMed 29702128](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29702128/)); about 8–10 exposures are needed ([PMC5438436](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5438436/)), yet parents typically give up after 3–4 ([Institut du goût](https://www.institutdugout.fr/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Education-au-goût-Pratiques-en-Nutrition.pdf)). That gap is a safe, useful job for an app. Cooking together increases picky eaters' willingness to try food ([Canada's Food Guide](https://food-guide.canada.ca/en/healthy-eating-recommendations/cook-more-often/involve-others-in-planning-and-preparing-meals/involving-kids-in-planning-and-preparing-meals/); [Manger Bouger](https://www.mangerbouger.fr/manger-mieux/se-faire-plaisir-en-mangeant-equilibre/cuisiner-maison/pourquoi-et-comment-cuisiner-avec-les-enfants-decouvrez-nos-idees-de-recettes)), and food should never be a reward or punishment ([Québec.ca](https://www.quebec.ca/sante/alimentation/saines-habitudes-alimentaires/alimentation-des-enfants)). The notes found no existing product that implements a parent-run, number-free family mode, though the scan was not exhaustive.

### Recommendation: a parent-operated "household" layer, not a child profile

The recommended design keeps the adult as the only user and adds a household layer to the adult's log. A family meal is logged once at meal level ("lentil curry, rice, salad"); the adult's own portion can carry grams in their own log, but nothing about any child's amount is ever recorded. The household view shows only non-numeric, parent-controllable structure: variety of food groups and plants across the week's family meals, the number of shared meals, and a "new food journey" that counts **offers, never intake** ("offered 4 times; 8–15 calm tries is normal"). Prompts come from the guidance above: cook-together tasks by age, let the child choose a new vegetable at the shop, serve family-style, screens off, no dessert as reward. An optional child-facing corner, if built at all, stays playful and sensory (taste-detective activities in the style of the French Classes du goût, build-a-plate games), drawing on the evidence that games can raise fruit and vegetable intake while snack advergames raise snack intake ([Deakin review](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Can_games_change_children_s_eating_behaviour_A_review_of_gamification_and_serious_games/20644836)).

The concrete guardrails follow from Kurbo and the expert consensus. There are no calories, grams, nutrient bars, weight, BMI or targets attached to or shown to any child, no red/green or good/bad labels in child-facing content, no exercise framed as earning food, no food or screen time as a reward for eating, no bite counters or "clean plate" goals, no photos of children, and no child login or self-signup. The coach, rule-based or LLM, refuses to comment on a child's weight, body or amount eaten and gives neutral signposting to a paediatrician or registered dietitian when a parent raises concern. Children with medical conditions are explicitly out of scope. Household members are identified by nickname at most, with an optional age band (for example "4–8") used only to pick age-appropriate cooking tasks.

This requires a deliberate change to the v1 rule. Today a static check bans child profiles and age gates. The recommendation is to replace that blanket ban with a narrower, still-enforced one: **no child user, no child-linked numeric data, no age gate**, and a static check that household entities cannot hold grams, nutrients or weight fields and cannot be sent to the AI provider, the sync server or the instance proxy. An age gate is deliberately not recommended: a self-declared gate is exactly the bypassable mechanism that failed Kurbo, and adding one would create "actual knowledge" of child users. Teenagers who use the app themselves remain outside its intended audience; the adult-directed positioning, absence of child-oriented branding and the calm-mode safeguards are the mitigation, and that residual risk should be stated openly.

### Legal implications (not legal advice)

**This section summarises research notes and law-firm commentary. It is not legal advice; a lawyer in each target jurisdiction should review any family mode before it ships.**

The amended COPPA Rule was published 22 April 2025, took effect 23 June 2025, and required full compliance by 22 April 2026. It adds biometric identifiers to personal information, requires a **separate verifiable parental consent before disclosing a child's data to third parties, with AI training never counted as integral**, and requires a written retention policy and a written security programme ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule); [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/05/childrens-online-privacy-in-2025-the-amended-coppa-rule)). Two FTC FAQ answers shape the design: COPPA covers data collected online *from* a child, not information a parent provides (A.8), and an app that only uses data stored on the device and never transmits it is not "collecting" (F.5) ([FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)). A parent-operated, local-only household layer therefore sits in the lowest-risk position. Child-oriented branding or marketing would count as evidence that the service is "directed to children" under the new factors. Sending a child-linked photo or text to an AI provider through the user's own key is the point where the profile changes most, which is why household data must never reach the AI path.

Under the GDPR, Art. 8 requires parental consent for information-society services offered directly to children under the national age, which ranges from 13 to 16 (15 in France, 16 in Germany, per a secondary table that should be checked against national law) ([GDPR Art. 8](https://rgpd.com/gdpr/chapter-2-principles/article-8-conditions-applicable-to-childs-consent-in-relation-to-information-society-services); [presencis](https://presencis.com/regulations/gdpr/article-8/)). Meal logs combined with goals, weight, allergies or conditions likely reveal health status and fall under Art. 9 special-category rules, which need explicit consent and probably a DPIA for children's data; the Art. 9 and 35 text was not re-fetched, so this is an inference from the regulation's structure ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Recital 18 exempts a family's purely household processing, but **not anyone who "provides the means"** for others ([Recital 18](https://luxgap.com/lois/rgpd/cons-18/?lang=en)). The CNIL says a party that only provides code has no controller role, and the household exemption may apply to health apps storing data locally without external connection ([Simmons & Simmons on CNIL](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cm3x6ddmg03zqtr0k9i1261m7/cnil-s-mobile-app-review-balancing-innovation-and-data-protection)). The practical reading: the upstream project is likely outside scope, a household self-hosting for itself likely inside the exemption, and a self-hoster running sync or the Phase 4 AI proxy for other families becomes a controller of potentially children's health data.

Other regimes point the same way. The UK Children's Code applies its 15 standards, including high-privacy defaults, no detrimental nudges and profiling off by default, to services "likely to be accessed" by children ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)). Washington's My Health My Data Act has no size threshold and a private right of action ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/02/First-Lawsuit-Filed-Under-Washingtons-My-Health-My-Data-Act)), which matters for any self-hoster serving Washington residents. The EU AI Act's Art. 5(1)(b) bans AI that exploits age-related vulnerabilities, including addictive reward schedules aimed at children, and Art. 50 has required disclosure that users are talking to an AI since 2 August 2026 ([Arthur Cox](https://www.arthurcox.com/knowledge/the-eu-commission-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices/); [CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-article-50-transparency-20260729/)). The FDA's January 2026 General Wellness guidance keeps healthy-eating support outside the device definition only while the intended purpose avoids treating or preventing disease ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)); weight targets for children would push toward treating obesity as a disease. The number-free design keeps the app on the wellness side of every one of these lines. One open question the notes did not resolve: AI provider terms on minors and data retention, which must be checked per provider before any household feature touches AI.

## Conclusion

The competitive scan changes what "AI nutrition coach" should mean for this project. AI logging is a commodity that every vendor sells and every vendor gets wrong in the same direction, so our edge is not the model but what surrounds it: grams with ranges, nutrients from open curated tables, and a coach that turns those numbers into a single reason and a single next step. The most valuable features in Phases 2 and 3 are cheap (a provenance tag, a hidden-fat question, a weekly "one thing to try"), and the most expensive one, an adaptive estimate, is optional. Making calorie reduction a by-product of density, plate balance and hidden fats, rather than a daily deficit, lets the app help adults eat less without becoming the tool that 73% of a clinical sample blamed.

The family mode is the less obvious opportunity. Every serious source agrees on what helps children, and none of it involves numbers, which means the best children's feature is a parent's coach for structure, exposure and shared meals. Built as a household layer on an adult's local log, it avoids the conditions that made Kurbo both a public-health controversy and a COPPA case, but only if the "no numbers about children" rule is enforced in code with the same rigour the project already applies to streaks and red states.
